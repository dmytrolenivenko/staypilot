A gap is the difference in median €/m² between two places close enough to be a real choice. This is where the money is: a border you cannot see on a price list.

Click any row to open it. These tables are wide, and reading a comparison across ten columns is the wrong shape for it — inside, the two places sit side by side with the listings behind each one, and the gap is worked out as money on a flat of the size that place actually sells. A rate per square metre is the honest way to compare two places and a useless way to picture the decision.

Distances are measured between the middle point of each place's listings, not between real borders — we hold no boundaries. So read 3 km as roughly how far you would move, not as an exact figure. Two places can also sit close on the map and still be separated by water or a motorway, which no distance here knows about.

Around one place shows every neighbour, including the ones priced much the same — “what is around Lisbon” means everything around it. Biggest gaps starts at a 20% floor instead, because every pair 1% apart across a whole level is thousands of rows of noise. Both floors live in Thresholds, and both can be changed.

Every place names itself with the places it sits inside underneath, and a chip saying which grain it is: a distrito is the broadest, a município is a council inside one, a freguesia is a parish inside that. Both halves of a pair are always the same grain — Level sets it, and it decides what counts as a neighbour at all.

Compare is the control that makes a gap trustworthy. On all stock, two places can sit 30% apart purely because one of them sells villas and the other sells studios — that gap is a fact about the buildings, not about the places, and moving would save you nothing. Pick a typology and it compares T2 against T2 instead. The all stock column stays on screen either way, because the two side by side are the real finding: “T2s are 30% apart while the places overall are within 4%” says something neither number says alone.
