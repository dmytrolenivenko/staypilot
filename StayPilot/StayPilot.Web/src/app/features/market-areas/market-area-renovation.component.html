How the discount is worked out. Every listing in a place is sorted into one of three piles. A project is one whose advert says it needs work, or whose energy certificate is D or worse. Finished means the advert calls it good, renovated, or a new build. Everything else — an unknown condition with no certificate — goes in neither pile and is counted separately. The discount is the middle €/m² of the finished pile minus the middle €/m² of the project pile. That is the whole calculation: one subtraction between two medians, from real adverts.

Why the certificate does most of the work. “Needs renovation” is set on barely 1% of listings — too few for most places to have any project stock at all. A poor energy grade covers roughly ten times as many and is measured rather than typed by an agent. It is also not quite the same claim: a grade E flat is badly insulated, which overlaps with needing work without being identical to it. Open any row to see which of the two signals its projects came from.

Trust is why this screen is worth believing at all. Two medians always differ by something. Whether that something is a finding depends on how many adverts each side rests on, how far the two price spreads sit apart, and how much of the place's stock got sorted at all. Where the project prices and the finished prices cover the same ground, the gap between their middles is noise wearing a decimal point — and the row says so, with the reason.

Cost is an estimate, and yours to set. It is kept in its own column and never merged into the measured discount, because one is read off the market and the other is a guess — when the guess is wrong you need to be able to see it.
